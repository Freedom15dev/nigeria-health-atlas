Halima is 52. Her kidneys have failed, so she needs dialysis 2 to 3 times a week, for life. She lives in Bauchi.

(Halima is an illustration, not a real person. Her state came out of the data.)

Half of Bauchi's people live more than 72 km, in a straight line, from a dialysis unit the evidence says is working. Each session costs about ₦45,000 at a public hospital, paid by the family. That's ₦4.7m a year at two sessions a week.

I built a public-data atlas to ask: where in Nigeria is specialist care missing, what exactly is missing, who can pay, and what would one centre deliver?

What I found:
• Hospitals are spread very unevenly: Anambra has about 21x as many per person as Katsina.
• Published counts don't agree. National dialysis centre counts run from 80 to 200+. Working radiotherapy machines: 4 to 'fewer than 20', against 120 needed.
• When sources say why a unit fails, it's most often systems: power, water, consumables, maintenance. Not just missing machines.
• The states with the fewest hospitals are also the least able to pay (rank correlation −0.52).
• One illustrative dialysis centre would treat 45 people, but at public prices it receives ₦41,700 a session and needs ₦61,452 to break even.

The biggest lesson: public data tells you where to look first. It can't tell you what works on the ground. That check has to happen in person.

Every number is sourced and dated, and conflicting figures are kept side by side.
